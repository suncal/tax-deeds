Profit model

Assumptions — change these and every profit number updates

Ordinary US flip assumptions, not scraped facts. Buy costs cover deed, recording, transfer tax and a quiet-title budget — on tax deeds quiet title is the line people forget, usually $1,500–$3,500.

How each number is computed

  • Discount % = (published market value − asking price) ÷ market value. Both scraped.
  • Est. ARV = market value × the multiplier. The government value is as-is at a past date, so the multiplier covers repair uplift and appreciation since then.
  • All-in = price + buy costs + rehab + 12 months holding.
  • Net profit = ARV × (1 − selling costs) − all-in.
  • 70% rule = ARV × 0.70 − rehab. Pay under it and you have room for surprises.

The caveat that matters more than the model

A floor price row is showing a statutory minimum, not a quote. Mississippi publishes accumulated taxes and fees; the Secretary of State sets the actual sale price and can require an appraisal on a valuable parcel. So a Mississippi row at 99% off is telling you the taxes owed are trivial next to the county's value — not that the state will sell it for that. Texas minimum bids and the Pennsylvania $1,000 are real, quotable numbers.

State playbook

Set your discount filter honestly

19% below market is far too loose a filter for over-the-counter tax property — it lets in almost everything, and 19% is not a margin that survives tax-deed risk. In this data of parcels with a published value already clear 19%, and clear 50%. The scarce thing is not the discount; it is a parcel that is cheap and has clean title, real access and a buyer. Deals worth your time start around 70%.

Mississippi — the best source here

  • What you buy: land the state took after tax forfeiture and holds in inventory.
  • Why it wins: the only source publishing county market value, a street address, acreage and a blighted-structure flag. You can screen it properly without leaving this page.
  • How: apply to purchase online through the Secretary of State. No auction.
  • Price: accumulated taxes and fees are the floor; the SoS sets the final price and may require an appraisal. Treat the discount as indicative, then call for a real number.
  • Coverage: parcels, counties, flagged blighted.

Texas — where the discount is provable

  • What you buy: struck-off property. It went to auction, nobody bid, so the taxing units hold it in trust and resell privately.
  • Why it is trustworthy: Texas requires the judgment to state an appraised value, printed beside the minimum bid. The bid is a real, quotable price.
  • The lever most people miss: once redemption expires you may offer less than the posted minimum. Every taxing unit must approve — 60–180 days — but that is where deep discounts happen.
  • Redemption: 2 years homestead/agricultural, 6 months otherwise, and the owner redeems at a 25–50% premium — so being redeemed is a paid outcome, not a loss.
  • Watch for: mineral and working-interest rows. No surface, no house, no exit. Filtered out by default.

Alabama — cheapest entry in the country

  • What you buy: the state's tax certificate, or a tax deed once the state has held it 3+ years.
  • How: no auction, no competition. Pick parcels off the county transcript, submit to the Dept. of Revenue, pay. Any business day.
  • Price: amount bid at tax sale plus 12%/yr statutory interest and fees. Median base .
  • Redemption: 3 years from the sale date.
  • The catch: no address, no value, no condition — a parcel ID and a legal description. That is exactly why it stays cheap: unscreenable at scale, so almost nobody screens it.
  • Verify in ~2 min: parcel ID → county revenue commissioner's property search → appraised value, improvement record, acreage.

Pennsylvania — $1,000 flat, with an address

  • What you buy: repository property — failed the upset sale and the judicial sale, so it sells free and clear of delinquent taxes and liens. Genuinely rare.
  • How: bid form to the Tax Claim Bureau any time. Delaware County starts at $1,000 plus a $250 demolition/rehab fee.
  • The catch: of the list is GRD — ground only, much of it unbuildable slivers behind other houses.
  • Approval: county, municipality and school district must all consent. Expect weeks.

Land lots vs. homes — which to actually buy

  • Land, $200–$2,000: no rehab, no holding risk, no eviction, no insurance. Small margin per deal, but the failure mode is losing $500, not $50,000.
  • Homes at a deep discount: far higher upside, far higher risk. Sight-unseen, no interior access, a value that may be years stale, and you may inherit an occupant.
  • Blighted homes: the flag is a warning, not a bargain signal. Blight can carry municipal demolition liens that survive the sale. Price the teardown, not the house.
  • The reliable unglamorous one: a landlocked strip is worth little to the market and a lot to the one neighbour it adjoins. Many slivers have exactly one rational buyer — a feature, at $1,000.

Where the profit comes from

  • The spread between a tax-derived price and a market value, captured on resale.
  • Redemption interest. Alabama pays 12%/yr; if it redeems you get your money back plus interest.
  • Volume on cheap land. At a $196 median, a hundred Alabama parcels costs less than one bad flip.
  • Being early. These lists refresh faster than most buyers check them — the whole point of re-scraping this page daily.

Risks & due diligence

What actually goes wrong

  • Redemption. The former owner takes it back. Know the window before you buy.
  • Title. A tax deed is not insurable title. Budget a quiet-title action. The most common reason a cheap deal stops being cheap.
  • Stale values. Government values are set at a past date — sometimes 20 years ago in the Texas data.
  • Unbuildable or unreachable. No road frontage, no utilities, wetlands, a 10-foot sliver, an undivided fractional interest. Common in every list here.
  • Surviving liens. Tax sales wipe most liens, but IRS liens carry a 120-day federal redemption right, and municipal, demolition and HOA claims can survive depending on state.
  • Occupancy. Somebody may be living there. You inherit the eviction.
  • Post-judgment taxes. Texas rows saying "subject to post-judgment years 20XX forward" mean those unpaid years are yours on top of the bid.
  • Mobile homes. Often titled separately from the land — you can buy the dirt and not the house.

Screening order that saves the most time

  • 1. Pull the parcel on the county GIS or appraisal district. Confirm it exists, has road frontage, check the improvement record.
  • 2. Look at it on satellite and street view. Thirty seconds kills most of the list.
  • 3. Read the current appraised value and last sale price against the asking price here.
  • 4. Confirm redemption has expired, or price the wait.
  • 5. Ask the county whether it is still available — lists lag reality by days to weeks.
  • 6. Only then run the profit model, and only then send a bid form.

Tried and blocked — real programs no automated feed can reach

Live OTC programs with genuine inventory that refuse scripted access. Worth checking by hand.

  • Florida — "Lands Available for Taxes." County-held deeds that failed auction; after 90 days anyone may buy at the opening bid. Structurally the best OTC deed program in the US. Most counties run on RealTDM, which 403s everything scripted — including a real browser session. Brevard · Manatee · Orange
  • Arizona — state CP certificates. Downloadable list behind a Blazor app. Liens, not deeds. Maricopa Treasurer
  • Arkansas — Commissioner of State Lands post-auction sales. Statewide; unsold parcels available 30 days after auction via an online portal. cosl.org
  • Texas — other firms. Linebarger (taxsales.lgbs.com, refuses non-browser connections) and MVBA (reachable, but 3 lists in 3 formats — not worth a fragile parser).
  • More Pennsylvania repositories. Delaware is 1 of ~60 counties. Northumberland and Clinton start at $500, Monroe $800, Dauphin $2,000 — most publish only a phone number. Westmoreland · Clinton
