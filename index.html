Assumptions — change these and the deal table recalculates instantly

Defaults are ordinary US flip assumptions, not scraped facts. Buy costs cover deed, recording, transfer tax and a quiet-title budget — on tax deeds quiet title is the line people forget, and it is usually $1,500–$3,500 with an attorney.

How each column is computed

  • Discount % = (published market value − asking price) ÷ published market value. Both numbers scraped.
  • Est. ARV = published market value × the multiplier above. The government value is an as-is value at a past date, so the multiplier covers both repair uplift and appreciation since then. Texas judgment dates in this data run from 2005 to 2026 — on an old judgment the value is stale and probably understates today.
  • All-in = price + buy costs + rehab + 12 months holding.
  • Net profit = ARV × (1 − selling costs) − all-in.
  • 70% rule = ARV × 0.70 − rehab. Pay under this and you have room for surprises.

One caveat that matters more than the model

A floor price badge means the published number is a statutory minimum, not a quote. Mississippi publishes the accumulated tax and fee total; the Secretary of State sets the actual sale price and can require an appraisal on a valuable parcel. So a Mississippi row showing 99% off is telling you the taxes owed are trivial next to the county's value — it is not telling you the state will sell it for that. Texas minimum bids and the Pennsylvania $1,000 are real, quotable numbers.

The one thing to fix in the brief

19% below market is far too loose a filter for over-the-counter tax property — it lets in almost everything, and 19% is not a margin that survives tax-deed risk. In this scraped data of parcels with a published value already clear 19%, and clear 50%. The scarce thing is not the discount; it is a parcel that is cheap and has clean title, real access and a buyer. The slider starts at your 19% — the deals worth your time start around 70%.

Mississippi — the best source in this dashboard

  • What you buy: land the state took after tax forfeiture and now holds in inventory.
  • Why it is the best: it is the only source that publishes the county market value, a street address, acreage and a blighted-structure flag. You can screen it properly without leaving this page.
  • How: apply to purchase online through the Secretary of State. No auction, no bidding war.
  • Price: the accumulated taxes and fees are the floor. The SoS sets the final price and may require an appraisal on a valuable parcel — treat the discount as indicative, then ring them for a real number.
  • Coverage: parcels across counties, flagged blighted.

Texas — where the discount is provable

  • What you buy: "struck-off" property. It went to auction, nobody bid, so the taxing units hold it in trust and resell it privately.
  • Why the numbers are trustworthy: Texas requires the judgment to state an appraised value, and the lists print it beside the minimum bid. The bid is a real, quotable price.
  • The lever most people miss: once redemption has expired you may offer less than the posted minimum. Every taxing unit must approve, which takes 60–180 days — but that is where deep discounts happen.
  • Redemption: 2 years for homestead and agricultural land, 6 months otherwise — and the owner redeems at a 25–50% premium, so being redeemed is a paid outcome, not a loss.
  • Watch for: mineral and working-interest rows. Cheap, bargain-looking, and there is no surface, no house and no exit. Filtered out by default.

Alabama — the cheapest entry in the country

  • What you buy: the state's tax certificate, or a tax deed once the state has held it 3+ years.
  • How: no auction, no competition. Pick parcels off the county transcript, submit to the Dept. of Revenue, pay. Any business day.
  • Price: the amount bid at tax sale plus 12%/yr statutory interest and fees. Median base here is .
  • Redemption: 3 years from the sale date. Buy certificates already past that window if you want the property rather than the interest.
  • The catch: no address, no value, no condition — a parcel ID and a legal description. That is exactly why it stays cheap: it is unscreenable at scale, so almost nobody screens it.
  • Verify in ~2 min: parcel ID → the county revenue commissioner's property search → appraised value, improvement record, acreage. That one step turns a row here into a deal or a pass.

Pennsylvania — $1,000 flat, with an address

  • What you buy: repository property — it failed the upset sale and the judicial sale, so it sells free and clear of delinquent taxes and liens. Genuinely rare in tax sales.
  • How: bid form to the Tax Claim Bureau any time. Delaware County starts at $1,000, plus a $250 demolition/rehab fee added 1 Jan 2026.
  • The catch, and it is real: of this list is flagged GRD — ground only, much of it unbuildable slivers behind other houses. The few with a street number and no GRD flag are the ones to look at.
  • Approval: county, municipality and school district must all consent. Expect weeks.

Land lots vs. homes — which to actually buy

  • Land, $200–$2,000: no rehab, no holding risk, no eviction, no insurance. Margin is small per deal but the failure mode is losing $500, not $50,000. Exit is owner-financed resale or the adjacent neighbour.
  • Homes at a deep discount: far higher upside, far higher risk. You are buying sight-unseen with no interior access, on a value that may be years stale, and you may inherit an occupant.
  • Blighted homes specifically: Mississippi's blighted flag is a warning, not a bargain signal. A blighted structure can carry municipal demolition liens that survive the sale, and the city may already have a demolition order. Price the teardown, not the house.
  • The unglamorous reliable one: a landlocked strip is worth little to the market and a lot to the one neighbour it adjoins. Many repository slivers have exactly one rational buyer — a feature, at $1,000.

Where the profit comes from

  • The spread between a tax-derived price and a market value, captured by reselling.
  • Redemption interest. In Alabama an unredeemed certificate pays 12%/yr; if it redeems you get your money back plus interest. Losing the property is still a paid outcome.
  • Volume on cheap land. At a $196 median, a hundred Alabama parcels costs less than one bad flip.
  • Being early. These lists refresh faster than most buyers check them, which is the whole point of re-scraping this page every day.

What actually goes wrong

  • Redemption. The former owner takes it back. Know the window in each state before you buy.
  • Title. A tax deed is not insurable title. Budget a quiet-title action or a tax-deed-friendly title company. This is the most common reason a cheap deal stops being cheap.
  • Stale values. Government values are set at a past date — sometimes 20 years ago in the Texas data. The discount is a starting point, not a fact about today's market.
  • Unbuildable or unreachable. No road frontage, no utilities, wetlands, a 10-foot sliver, an undivided fractional interest. Common in every one of these lists.
  • Surviving liens. Tax sales wipe most liens, but IRS liens carry a 120-day federal redemption right, and municipal, demolition and HOA claims can survive depending on state.
  • Occupancy. Somebody may be living there. You inherit the eviction.
  • Post-judgment taxes. Several Texas rows say "subject to post-judgment years 20XX forward" — those unpaid years are yours on top of the bid.
  • Mobile homes. Often titled separately from the land. You can buy the dirt and not the house.

Screening order that saves the most time

  • 1. Pull the parcel on the county GIS or appraisal district. Confirm it exists, has road frontage, and check the improvement record for a structure.
  • 2. Look at it on satellite and street view. Thirty seconds kills most of the list.
  • 3. Read the current appraised value and last sale price. Compare with the asking price here.
  • 4. Confirm redemption has expired, or price the wait.
  • 5. Ask the county whether it is still available — lists lag reality by days to weeks.
  • 6. Only then run the profit model, and only then send a bid form.

Said plainly: this is a screening tool built from public records. It is not investment advice and I am not a licensed advisor. Verify every number against the county before committing money.

Tried and blocked — real programs an automated feed cannot reach

These are live OTC programs with genuine inventory. Each refused scripted access (403, or a portal that only renders in a logged-in browser), so they cannot be part of a daily job. Worth checking by hand.

  • Florida — "Lands Available for Taxes." County-held deeds that failed auction; after 90 days anyone may buy at the opening bid. Structurally the best OTC deed program in the US. Most counties run on RealTDM, which returns 403 to everything scripted, including a real browser session. Brevard · Manatee · Orange
  • Arizona — state CP certificates. Maricopa publishes a downloadable State CP list behind a Blazor app. These are liens, not deeds. Maricopa Treasurer
  • Arkansas — Commissioner of State Lands post-auction sales. Statewide; unsold parcels become available 30 days after auction, now through an online auction portal. cosl.org
  • Texas — other firms. Linebarger (taxsales.lgbs.com, refuses non-browser connections) and MVBA (trust properties — reachable, but only 3 lists and each in a different format, so not worth a fragile parser).
  • More Pennsylvania repositories. Delaware is 1 of ~60 counties. Northumberland and Clinton start at $500/parcel, Monroe $800, Dauphin $2,000 — most publish only a phone number or a paywalled list. Westmoreland · Clinton
